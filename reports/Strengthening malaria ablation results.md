# Bound the nulls before chasing gains

The fastest honest way to strengthen this project is to re-analyse predictions the team already has, not to train new models. Three zero-cost steps change what the paper can claim. First, the one "significant" result has to be restated. The fold-level leakage test (Welch p = 0.0075, Holm 0.023) treated ten folds that share training data as independent; a Nadeau–Bengio-style correction moves it to **p = 0.048, Holm p = 0.14, 95 % CI −3.28 to −0.02 pp MCC**. Second, the saved predictions allow a **paired leakage test on identical cells**: 1,095 shared hold-out cells, or 17,591 shared out-of-fold cells. Third, exploratory equivalence tests turn H2's rejection into a bounded result: on unseen patients, YUV luma equalisation changed ensemble MCC by **−0.76 to +0.15 pp (90 % CI), inside a ±1 pp margin**.

Model-side options promise little for a 98.45 % same-hospital ensemble. Colour augmentation is the evidence-backed alternative to normalisation, but its documented gains come across sites, and D4 test-time augmentation would flip only a handful of the 5,550 hold-out cells. Two free analyses add genuinely new content. One is a pre-specified patient-level diagnosis rule, reported with caveats because the crops are enriched. The other is an error audit that starts from the fact that **30 of A1's 31 false alarms come from infected patients' smears**. External validation on MP-IDB is the only experiment that tests H2's underlying idea, but it costs 3–5 person-days and should show a double-digit drop.

The documents must stop calling the leakage effect "measurable" and stop generalising about "stain normalisation" from a luma-only step. The essential work fits in about 2–3 person-days and none of the $7.90 RunPod balance. One optional $2–3 training arm is defensible only with a pre-registered equivalence margin.

## Re-analysing saved predictions is the strongest upgrade

The team's strongest asset is procedural. The analysis plan was committed at 03:24 on 2 November 2026, before any A1–A3 model was trained, and the results followed at 05:00 ([analysis plan](../docs/phase3_analysis_plan.md)). The way the results are now summarised partly undercuts that discipline. One p-value overstates the evidence, both nulls are described as absences rather than bounds, and the paper calls "measurable" a leakage effect that the design could barely see. Varoquaux and Cheplygina's warning cuts both ways: "the lack of statistically significant results does not demonstrate the absence of effect", yet "any trivial effect can be significant given enough data" ([Varoquaux & Cheplygina 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-022-00592-y)). Every fix in this section runs on files already in the repository.

### Correcting for shared training data lifts Holm p from 0.023 to 0.14

The H1 fold-level contrast compares a mean fold MCC of 93.92 for A1 (patient split) with 95.57 for A0 (image split), a difference of **−1.65 pp** ([ablation summary](../results/ablation/ablation_summary.md)). Because the two arms use different folds, the analysis code falls back to a plain Welch t-test on the fold scores, in the unpaired branch of [`src/ablation_stats.py`](../src/ablation_stats.py). The plan itself called that test "descriptive only", yet its result (Welch p = 0.0075, Holm 0.023) now appears as evidence in the README, the report, the IEEE abstract, Section IV and the contrast table.

Ten cross-validation folds share most of their training data, so their scores are correlated. On such scores the usual t-test has a Type I error rate "much larger than α", which the Nadeau–Bengio heuristic exists to correct ([Benavoli et al. 2017](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-305.html)). Inflating each arm's fold variance by the 10-fold factor (1/k + n_test/n_train)/(1/k) ≈ 2.11 gives **p = 0.048, a 95 % CI of −3.28 to −0.02 pp, and a Holm-adjusted p of 0.14** across the three contrasts. Every H1 fold-level interval in the IEEE table widens by about 45 %; accuracy's −0.54 pp, for example, becomes roughly −1.27 to +0.19. Only the MCC interval still excludes zero, by 0.02 pp, and none survives Holm.

The fix belongs in the code, so that tables, figures and slides regenerate together rather than being hand-edited. The replacement for the report and Section IV of the paper:

> Cross-validation over the 160 non-hold-out patients gives a point estimate of −1.65 pp in mean fold MCC for the patient split (93.92 vs 95.57; 95 % CI −3.28 to −0.02 after a Nadeau–Bengio-style correction for overlapping training sets; p = 0.048, Holm-adjusted p = 0.14). An earlier uncorrected Welch test (p = 0.0075, Holm 0.023) treated the ten folds as independent and overstated the evidence.

The abstract can shorten this to "cross-validation over the remaining 160 patients suggests a small leakage effect (fold MCC −1.65 pp, 95 % CI −3.28 to −0.02; Holm-adjusted p = 0.14), with 2.5 times the fold-to-fold spread". Applying the correction to each arm separately is itself a heuristic, because Nadeau and Bengio derived it for paired splits. The paired test described below is the principled replacement.

Restated, the estimate is still worth reporting. Its accuracy drop of 0.54 pp nearly matches the slide-disjoint drop Hou et al. measured for EfficientNet-B0 (0.47 pp), and it sits inside their 0.25–1.45 pp range across eight architectures. They also note that no earlier work had quantified slide leakage on this benchmark ([Hou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios16070358)). It is about a fifth of the 2.7 pp gap between cell-level and patient-level evaluation in the dataset's original paper ([Rajaraman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.4568)). It is also orders of magnitude below slice-level leakage in brain MRI, which inflated accuracy by 29–55 % ([Yagis et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-01681-w)). A plausible but untested reading is that the label is visible in the cell itself, so patient identity adds little exploitable signal.

The pre-registered hold-out test of H1 could not have detected an effect of this size. Comparing two different hold-out sets is unpaired, which by the research notes' calculation puts its minimum detectable effect near **2.2 pp MCC**. On top of that, the 40-patient draw is about 1 pp easier than an average group of unseen patients: the same models score 98.22 % on it but 97.24 % in cross-validation. The honest verdict on H1 is "inconclusive at the hold-out, small and fragile in cross-validation", not "no leakage".

### A paired test on 1,095 identical cells costs nothing

The saved predictions make a much cleaner leakage test possible. A0's image-split hold-out contains **1,095 cells (525 parasitized) from A1's 40 held-out patients** ([per-patient table](../results/ablation/ablation_per_patient.csv)). On exactly those cells, A0's ensemble was trained with other cells from the same patients and A1's ensemble never saw them. Comparing the two ensembles cell by cell, with a paired patient-cluster bootstrap over the 40 patients, removes both the easier-hold-out confound and the unpaired variance that sank H1's test. It is the same-cells version of the record-wise versus subject-wise comparison that Saeb et al. used to show how much record-wise validation can overstate accuracy ([Saeb et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019)).

Its weakness is size. Scaling the A2−A1 paired standard error (0.276 pp on 5,550 cells) by cell count puts the minimum detectable effect at roughly 1.7 pp MCC or more, so this test can come back inconclusive. The bootstrap will give the real figure.

The out-of-fold predictions supply a larger version. Both arms saved per-cell predictions for their cross-validation folds, and **17,591 cells from all 160 cross-validation patients** appear in both sets. Each of these cells was scored once by an A0 fold model that had seen other cells of that patient, and once by an A1 fold model that had never seen the patient. That is 16 times as many cells and four times as many patients as the hold-out version. Its interval conditions on one training run per fold, so it understates seed-to-seed noise. Bouthillier et al. found data sampling to be the dominant source of variance in benchmark comparisons, which suggests the understatement is modest ([Bouthillier et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)).

Before computing either comparison, the team should pre-register which comparison is primary (the ensemble version matches H1's estimand), MCC as the metric, and a ±1 pp equivalence margin, so that "small" becomes testable in both directions. Each comparison takes 2–4 person-hours.

### Equivalence bounds turn the YUV null into a finding

Rejecting H2 by the pre-registered rule says only that YUV did not deliver a gain as large as A1's fold SD of 1.51 pp. An equivalence test says what the data rule out. Under two one-sided tests (TOST), an effect counts as equivalent when its 90 % confidence interval lies inside a stated, justified margin ([Lakens 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550617697177)). A margin of ±1 pp MCC is about ±0.5 pp accuracy, or 28 hold-out cells. That is half of the ±1 % accuracy "region of practical equivalence" that Benavoli et al. propose for classifier comparisons ([Benavoli et al. 2017](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-305.html)). The research notes computed the tests below from the reported intervals using normal approximations; the team should recompute them from its 10,000 bootstrap replicates.

| Contrast | ΔMCC (pp) | 90 % CI (pp) | Equivalent within ±1 pp? | TOST p at ±1 pp |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A2 − A1, hold-out ensemble, paired | −0.26 | ≈ −0.76 to +0.15 | **Yes** | ≈ 0.007 |
| A3 − A0, hold-out ensemble, paired | 0.00 | ≈ −0.44 to +0.45 | **Yes** (also within ±0.5) | ≈ 0.0001 |
| A2 − A1, ten paired folds, corrected t | −0.60 | −1.25 to +0.05 | No; yes within the pre-registered 1.51 | 0.14 (0.015 at ±1.51) |

Both hold-out contrasts pass even after Holm correction across the three tests. The fold contrast is undetermined at ±1 pp. A Bayesian correlated t-test on the same numbers puts about an 86 % posterior probability on a fold-level effect within ±1 pp. That supports the wording "the fold data favour practical equivalence but do not establish it". Because no margin was pre-registered, the paper should label these tests exploratory and report the smallest margin each contrast passes: about 0.76 pp for A2−A1 on the hold-out, 0.45 pp for A3−A0, and 1.25 pp for the folds.

Two sensitivity statements belong beside the tests and fill TRIPOD+AI item 10 on study size ([Collins et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078378)). By the same calculation, the paired hold-out design could detect about **0.77 pp MCC (≈0.4 pp accuracy, roughly 21 cells)** with 80 % power at two-sided α = 0.05. Paired folds could detect about 1.0–1.1 pp, and the unpaired H1 hold-out comparison only about 2.2 pp. These are properties of the design; "observed power" should never be reported.

The team can also measure what patient clustering costs by rerunning the hold-out bootstrap over cells instead of patients. The ratio of the two variances is the design effect, and it shows that precision depends on the 40 patients more than on the 5,550 cells. Hou et al. saw per-cell Wilson intervals of ±0.6 pp widen to ±1.2–3.3 pp under a slide-level bootstrap ([Hou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios16070358)).

### CLAIM 2024 and a dated addendum lock in the pre-registration

CLAIM 2024 is the closest reporting checklist for this study ([Tejani et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1148/ryai.240300)). Its item 20 asks at what level the partitions are disjoint, and says they "generally should be disjoint at the patient level or higher". The team can answer it better than most papers on this dataset. Patients are grouped by the file name's C-number; the grouping was checked against NIH's patient-to-cell mapping CSVs (200 patients, with patient C47's two microscope keys merged); and the code asserts that no patient crosses the split. The 193-patient figure on NLM's datasheet belongs to the separate full-image release, so it does not threaten this grouping ([NLM datasheet](https://lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/LHC-research/LHC-projects/image-processing/malaria-datasheet.html)). The same item should state that A0 and A3 are disjoint only at the image level.

The other CLAIM items that matter here are uncertainty (29), robustness analyses (30), external data (33, answered "none"), failure analysis (39), protocol (42) and code and models (43). TRIPOD+AI's calibration and heterogeneity items matter too. A model info sheet fits as a supplement: Kapoor and Narayanan's 21-question template for justifying the absence of leakage ([Kapoor & Narayanan 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804)). ## Colour augmentation and TTA promise near-zero same-site gains

### A2 equalised brightness, not stain colour

The largest controlled comparison favours colour augmentation over normalisation. Tellez et al. trained 760 networks on one centre and tested them in nine external laboratories ([Tellez et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06543)). The best configuration used **no normalisation with light HED colour augmentation** (mean rank 1.2 of 38), and all ten top configurations used HSV or HED augmentation. Normalisation helped only when colour augmentation was absent, and classical normalisers never beat a grayscale baseline. Boschman et al. found that normalisation "does not consistently improve classification performance when both training and testing data are from a single center", though it gained 0.21–0.25 AUC on multi-centre external sets ([Boschman et al.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6637be469eaf5ba1b9f5a133fcf4b29e/)). On the MIDOG22 benchmark, stain augmentation had the best F1 of 29 methods, 1.3 pp above plain training ([Jahanifar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19656)). Normalisation can even hurt: normalising in HED space cut a ResNet-18 by 10.2 pp on one external set ([Shen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12694)).

The malaria evidence points the same way. On the NIH cells, stain normalisation lowered accuracy by 0.3–2.4 pp for all three models in one preprint ([Rahman et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10418)). Macenko normalisation cut cross-scanner malaria detection from 91.0 to 72.0 mAP50 in another ([Xu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06592)). No malaria study ablates HSV or HED colour jitter, so its in-domain effect on this task is unmeasured.

A2's null therefore needs no special explanation; three reasons compound. First, the test patients come from the same hospital and staining protocol as the training patients, which is exactly the setting where normalisation shows no consistent benefit. Second, A2 equalises only the luma (Y) channel and leaves the chroma channels that carry stain hue untouched. It therefore normalises brightness and contrast, not stain colour, and the training pipeline already randomises those: a OneOf of CLAHE, sharpening, embossing, contrast and brightness is applied to 30 % of images ([`src/marques2022_exact.py`](../src/marques2022_exact.py)). Third, the headroom is small: 86 hold-out errors, part of them probably label disagreements (see below), against a minimum detectable effect of about 21 cells.

The documents should call A2 "luma histogram equalisation (the stain normalisation of Umer et al.)". Otherwise readers will take H2 as a test of stain-colour normalisation, which it was not. A2's specificity dip (−0.44 pp, 95 % CI −0.92 to −0.04, Holm p = 0.118) and its extra false alarms (43 versus 31) are not a finding. They do justify a visual check of 20–30 equalised crops, since about a quarter of a typical crop is black background ([unpublished capstone measurement](https://github.com/noahtjonsk/malaria-cross-dataset)) and a global equalisation on such crops can stretch cell interiors unpredictably.

### One HED-light arm is worth $2–3 only with an equivalence margin

If the team spends GPU money on one more hypothesis, Tellez's HED-light augmentation is the best-supported choice. It decomposes each crop with scikit-image's `rgb2hed`, scales each stain channel by U(0.95, 1.05) and shifts it by U(−0.05, 0.05), then converts back with `hed2rgb`, keeping the black background black ([Tellez et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06543); [scikit-image](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/api/skimage.color.html)). That is about 15 lines inside an albumentations `Lambda`, applied with a pre-specified probability; 0.5 is a choice, not a literature value. Check it on about 50 augmented training cells, never test cells, to confirm that parasite chromatin stays visible. HSV jitter through `HueSaturationValue` needs no new code but is a weaker fallback: Tellez's HSV-light setting ranked only 17.4 of 38 without normalisation.

The honest expectation is no measurable gain. The research notes put the plausible same-site effect at −0.5 to +0.3 pp MCC, against minimum detectable effects of 0.77 pp for the ensemble and 1.1 pp for the folds. Power to detect a true +0.3 pp gain is therefore only 10–20 %. The arm is worth its **$2–3, 15 minutes of GPU time and 2–4 person-hours** only as a newly pre-registered H3 with an equivalence margin and four outcomes. It succeeds if the patient-bootstrap CI excludes zero and the Holm-adjusted fold test agrees. It shows equivalence if the 90 % CI lies within ±1 pp, which is achievable if the true difference is below about 0.55 pp. It shows harm if the CI lies entirely below zero, and it is inconclusive otherwise. Its value is to answer the examiner's obvious question, "why test normalisation rather than the augmentation the literature supports?", with a bounded null. Its documented benefit, robustness across sites, can only show up in an external test. If the team would rather spend the same $2–3 on statistics, rerunning A1 with new seeds measures the run-to-run noise floor that every ablation contrast must clear. Hou et al. report a seed SD of ±0.60 pp accuracy for EfficientNet-B0 under slide-disjoint splits ([Hou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios16070358)). With $7.90 left, one arm plus a reserve for a failed run is the sensible ceiling.

### D4 test-time augmentation would flip a handful of cells

Test-time augmentation (TTA) helps single models a little and ensembles barely at all. Averaging 30 flipped, cropped and rescaled views raised ImageNet ResNet-50 accuracy by 1.1 pp. However, for one ImageNet model "a little over one third" of the labels TTA changed were wrong, and the benefit shrank with larger networks ([Shanmugam et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.11156)). For 10-member deep ensembles on CIFAR-10, TTA changed error by −0.05 to +0.16 pp, making one ensemble worse ([Ashukha et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06470)). The only malaria-specific result found used single models on a random image split of the NIH cells and gained +0.25 and +0.48 pp ([Rahman et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10418)).

The team's models were trained with RandomRotate90, Flip and Transpose, so all eight D4 orientations are in-distribution, and averaging them makes each model exactly orientation-invariant. The research notes estimate a realistic ensemble gain of 0–0.1 pp accuracy, or 0–6 of the 5,550 cells. A related check already exists: the paper's augmented evaluation protocol, which scores one random augmented view of each cell, left all three verdicts unchanged.

D4 TTA is still a cheap robustness check. It needs 444,000 new forward passes per arm (8 views × 10 models × 5,550 cells), which run locally for free, and about 2–3 person-hours. It should report the change in ensemble MCC with the same patient bootstrap and the number of cells flipped in each direction. It should also report ECE, Brier score and log-loss, both raw and after temperature scaling fitted on out-of-fold predictions, never on the hold-out. Use orientations only, because the models never saw colour jitter and test-time transforms they were not trained on can hurt. A same-model comparison has a tiny paired standard error, so a three-cell change could reach "significance". Pre-specify a minimal important difference such as 0.5 pp MCC, and present the result as "D4 TTA did not change the conclusions", never as an improvement.

## Saved predictions support two genuinely new analyses

### A patient-level rule must be chosen on the 160 cross-validation patients

In the literature on this dataset, "patient-level" usually means a cell classifier evaluated under patient-grouped cross-validation, not a diagnosis. Rajaraman et al.'s 95.9 % "patient level" accuracy is a cell metric ([Rajaraman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.4568)), and Malaria Screener's thin-smear "Patient" row reproduces it ([Yu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-020-05453-1)). The only per-patient diagnosis analysis found on these cells is a 2026 preprint. It swept a rule of "at least τ positive cells" after the fact across all 200 patients ([Al Kafi et al. 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24793)). With τ = 1, only 7 of 50 healthy patients were called negative (14 % specificity); τ = 10 reached 100 % specificity at 87.3 % sensitivity. Field systems show the same weak point. A Sudanese field study's primary mean-score rule reached 51.1 % specificity, and only a post-study count rule raised it to 91.1 % ([Yu et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-023-04446-0)). A rule chosen before it sees its test patients would go beyond anything published on this dataset.

The team's numbers look excellent at first sight. At a cell threshold of 0.5, only one of the 12 healthy hold-out patients (C3) has a single false-alarm crop. Every one of the 28 infected patients contributes at least 3 parasitized crops (median about 50), so almost any count rule will score near-perfectly. Much of that ease comes from how the dataset was built: NIH kept about 68 uninfected crops per patient whatever the patient's status, but apparently every annotated parasitized cell. The research notes infer from the cell numbers in NIH's file names that the uninfected crops are about 6 % of each patient's roughly 1,180 segmented cells. NIH does not document this ([NLM datasheet](https://lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/LHC-research/LHC-projects/image-processing/malaria-datasheet.html)). If the inference holds, a healthy patient's false-alarm exposure is about 17 times smaller than it would be on the imaged fields. Delahunt et al. warn that object-level specificity "can be boosted arbitrarily by surplus easy distractors" and that ROC curves are "insufficient to assess patient-level accuracy" ([Delahunt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.01901)).

A leak-free protocol fixes this using only saved predictions. The team pre-registers a count rule, under which a patient is positive if at least τ crops have p ≥ t, over a small grid such as t ∈ {0.19, 0.5, 0.8, 0.95, 0.99} and τ from 1 to 15. It then selects (t, τ) on the out-of-fold predictions of the 160 cross-validation patients (122 infected, 38 healthy) with a written criterion, such as maximum Youden's J subject to at least 95 % cross-validated specificity. That copies Autoscope's practice of pre-specifying thresholds on validation samples to target over 90 % specificity ([Torres et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-018-2493-0)). A nested leave-one-fold-out measures how optimistic the selection is.

The chosen rule is then applied once to the 40 hold-out patients, and also to each fold model separately. Out-of-fold probabilities come from single models and hold-out probabilities from the ensemble, and thresholds behave differently across the two: the team's own recall-first threshold of 0.189 moved hold-out cell specificity from 98.87 % to 93.93 %. A dilution stress test, keeping only 1, 2, 3 or 5 parasitized crops per infected patient, shows how fast sensitivity falls. Crop counts per patient must never enter a rule, because "68 + k" leaks the label.

The intervals stay wide. With 12 healthy hold-out patients, 12 of 12 gives a Clopper–Pearson 95 % interval of 73.5–100 % and 11 of 12 gives 61.5–99.8 %; 28 of 28 infected gives 87.7–100 %. A clearly labelled pooled estimate over all 50 healthy patients (38 from nested cross-validation plus 12 from the hold-out) narrows 50 of 50 to 92.9–100 %.

Clinical benchmarks are context only. They include WHO Level 1 detection of at least 90 % ([Horning et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-021-03631-3)), and WHO's RDT criteria of a false-positive rate below 10 % with 75 % panel detection at 200 parasites/µL ([WHO](https://www.who.int/teams/global-malaria-programme/case-management/diagnosis/rapid-diagnostic-tests/selection-and-procurement)). A thin film cannot reach clinical detection limits: a limit of 50 parasites/µL needs about 5 × 10⁵ RBCs on a thin film, which Delahunt et al. call "unworkable" ([Delahunt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.01901)). The work takes 0.5–1 person-day, and its honest label is "exploratory patient-level aggregation on curated crops".

### Thirty of 31 false alarms come from infected patients' smears

A1's 86 hold-out errors split into 55 missed parasitized cells (1.95 %) and 31 false alarms (1.13 %). Split by patient status, the false alarms are far from uniform. **30 of the 1,913 uninfected crops from the 28 infected patients (1.57 %) were flagged, against 1 of the 822 from the 12 healthy patients (0.12 %).** That is a thirteen-fold difference, spread across 18 infected patients rather than one bad slide ([per-patient table](../results/ablation/ablation_per_patient.csv)).

That is exactly where a known kind of label error must live. When a pathologist reviewed one NIH model's 29 false positives, 38 % were judged to show "clear formation of malaria parasites while labelled as uninfected" ([Rahman et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10418)), and such a cell can exist only on an infected patient's slide. The pattern also fits genuinely ambiguous early rings or debris next to parasites. Without an expert, the two explanations cannot be separated. Errors also cluster by patient: half come from 7 of the 40 patients, 14 patients have none, and one patient (C180) accounts for 14 missed cells.

The label-noise literature for this dataset is thinner than its citations suggest. Fuhad et al. set aside 1,397 cells (5.07 %) as "falsely labeled and suspicious" after consulting one unnamed expert, and reported no accuracy on the original labels. Their corrected-data link now returns HTTP 404 ([Fuhad et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7277980/)). So the next step listed in the team's report and paper, re-scoring on Fuhad's cleaned labels, is impossible as written. The only independent re-read available is Michael Delves's relabelling on IEEE DataPort. It sorts cells into parasitised, uninfected, "weird", "bad segmentation" and "unsure" folders, but publishes no counts ([IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/open-access/malaria-dataset-thin-blood-smear-updated)).

The team's own result bounds the problem. If a fraction ε of hold-out labels were random errors the model cannot reproduce, accuracy could not exceed 1 − ε. So 98.45 % caps such noise at 1.55 %, and Fuhad's 5 % cannot be read as a 95 % ceiling. Borrowing Rahman's 38 % (95 % CI 23–56 %) suggests that roughly 20–48 of the 86 errors are label disagreements. Accuracy against corrected labels would then be no more than 98.80–99.32 %. "A quarter to a half" is the defensible range, and it rests on one small preprint.

The audit itself takes about two hours on a laptop. Confident learning needs only labels and out-of-sample probabilities, which the team has for every cell; run it separately on the out-of-fold and hold-out predictions ([cleanlab documentation](https://docs.cleanlab.ai/stable/cleanlab/filter.html)). With two classes, ranking by self-confidence, margin or loss gives the same order. Every flag is only a candidate. Human reviewers confirmed only 51 % of confident-learning flags across ten benchmark test sets ([Northcutt et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749)). Automated relabelling wrongly changed 0.7–10.7 % of correct labels in a 2025 retinal-imaging study ([Lin et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01424-x)). And label smoothing, which this pipeline uses, degrades confidence as an error signal ([Xia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14715)).

The human side of the audit is a blind triage. Galleries of all 55 missed cells and 31 false alarms are shuffled with correctly classified controls, and two team members sort them blind against a simple rubric: clear parasite, faint edge dot, stain precipitate or debris, platelet-like inclusion, segmentation problem, blur or colour cast, nothing visible, cannot tell. The result is category counts with Cohen's κ. Matching file names against the Delves folders adds an independent re-read in about 30 minutes. A reliability diagram belongs beside the ECE of 0.065, with temperature scaling fitted on out-of-fold predictions. Rerunning the A2−A1 and A3−A0 contrasts without the flagged cells tests whether the verdicts survive; if they do, the paper can say "conclusions are robust to excluding N candidate label errors". Together these satisfy CLAIM's failure-analysis item, which one review found met by none of 9 studies ([CLAIM adherence review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10249293/table/Tab5)).

## Only an external test can probe what H2 was really about

Every number in this project comes from one hospital in Chittagong ([Rajaraman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.4568)), and in malaria microscopy the site is the dominant shift. In one study, a network told NIH images from another hospital's with 95 % accuracy, and an NIH-trained detector fell from 89.2 % to 66.2 % image-level accuracy at that hospital ([Guillon et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08792)). H2's idea is that normalisation makes a model robust to staining differences. That can only be tested where staining differs, and the 40-patient hold-out does not provide that.

MP-IDB's *P. falciparum* subset is the right single external set: same species, stain and smear type, from a different laboratory. It carries an MIT licence, is about 3 GB, and holds 104 images from 10 smears ([MP-IDB](https://github.com/andrealoddo/MP-IDB-The-Malaria-Parasite-Image-Database-for-Image-Processing-and-Analysis)). About 95 % of its parasites are ring stages, like NIH's. It labels only parasites, so the team must build uninfected crops with its own RBC segmentation.

Published baselines set expectations. Under a slide-disjoint split, MalariaNet fell from 95.6 % on NIH to **79.5 ± 2.9 % accuracy and 0.865 AUC** on MP-IDB ([Hou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios16070358)). Eleven NIH-trained CNNs scored 32–69 % before fine-tuning on MP-IDB restored about 92.6 % ([Loddo et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951236/)). The team should expect a double-digit drop, driven largely by a decision threshold that no longer fits. It should report AUC alongside sensitivity, specificity and MCC at the frozen NIH threshold, and never re-threshold on the external data. It should bootstrap over the 10 smears, which keeps the intervals wide.

Three issues need handling in advance. The first is preprocessing, which moves results by more than ten points. In an unpublished capstone that ran this experiment, background masking raised MP-IDB sensitivity by 11–15 pp for two of three CNNs, yet lowered AUC on BBBC041 for all three ([capstone repository](https://github.com/noahtjonsk/malaria-cross-dataset)). Framing and masking must therefore be fixed before scoring, and results reported both ways.

The second is the choice of colour test. MP-IDB's stain colour is almost identical to NIH's (a red–blue stain index of +42.7 against +42.5 in the same capstone), so it tests geometry, sharpness and site rather than stain colour. BBBC041, a *P. vivax* set from Brazil and Thailand with a large colour shift (−24.0), is the stronger colour test but confounds species ([BBBC041](https://bbbc.broadinstitute.org/BBBC041)); MalariaNet scored 53.2 % accuracy and 18 % specificity there. Running the A0–A3 models (and A4, if trained) on both sets, with predictions written down first, separates "normalisation helps under colour shift" from "normalisation helps under any site shift".

The third is a leakage trap. NLM's full-image thin-smear release and its 2025 MIRA re-annotation come from the same Chittagong patients and photographs as the training crops, so they look external but would leak ([NLM ReadMe](https://data.lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/public/Malaria/NIH-NLM-ThinBloodSmearsPf/ReadMe.pdf); [Wilm et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18483)).

The cost is mostly person-time: about 3–5 days for MP-IDB, most of it spent building negatives and fair crops, and 2–3 more for BBBC041. Inference with the 40 existing models takes minutes and costs nothing. The resulting claim is modest: "a robustness probe on 10 smears from one other laboratory, not a clinical validation". It would still turn the limitation "no external test set" into a measured one. For a team that wants to finish soon, an acceptable alternative is to keep stating "no external validation" plainly (CLAIM item 33) and pre-specify the MP-IDB protocol as future work.

## Conclusion

The negative results are worth more than the documents currently let them be. Restated with equivalence bounds and minimum detectable effects, they say something few papers on this dataset can. On unseen patients from the same hospital, this ensemble's accuracy owes at most about a point to patient leakage, and luma equalisation changes MCC by less than one point either way. The leakage estimate, small as it is, independently replicates Hou et al.'s figure for the same backbone. A paired test on identical cells would make it the most direct leakage measurement on these images. The project gains more by reporting precisely what its data rule out than by chasing the 98.45 % figure.

Two findings move where the remaining uncertainty lies. False alarms concentrate in infected patients' smears, which suggests that part of the residual error is a labelling question no image transform can fix. That caps what any further "improvement" arm could show. And H2's real question, whether colour handling buys robustness across laboratories, lies outside a single-hospital design altogether. The next experiment worth money is therefore an external one, not another in-domain arm.
